## Intro
Summarising activities that happened between January and December of a year feels just as arbitrary as doing so for activities that happened between April and March. Using April-March, i.e., following the Indian Financial Year calendar, gives us two interesting benefits - we can use the annual report as the basis of the report that we need to file with the Indian Government Ministry of Corporate Affairs, and it sort of overlaps with the Indian Academic Year (July-May). So, going forward, we will continue to use April - March for our annual reports, and when you see us referring to 2025 in this report, we mean April 2025 - March 2026.

2025 was an interesting year for the FOSS United Foundation. We celebrated five years of FOSS United, and all of our experiments and community building started revealing patterns. For the first five years of our life, we were doing what we thought the community needed, and we focused on addressing what the community specifically asked us to do. This meant organising events, starting new FOSS communities across the country in the form of city communities or student clubs, and running IndiaFOSS and FOSS Hack. But over the past year, we started understanding what the community needs, instead of what they want. This is usually referred to as the XY Problem, i.e., the community needs X, but they ask for Y instead. We also started understanding the incentive structures that push the community to do what they do, e.g., our higher education system and college hiring push students to rack up Positions of Responsibility (PoR) instead of doing deep work. Why is this important? Because last year was also the year when we realised that a lot of our work was firefighting, e.g., untangling an awkward situation at a CityFOSS conference across multiple weeks, and we wanted to move from addressing the symptoms of the problem to addressing the root causes.

In this annual report, I will try my best to lay out our work during 2025 and how it helped us gain a deeper understanding of what the Indian FOSS community needs from us. And just to be clear, we are just one of the many FOSS, Free Software, and Open Source Software communities in India. Luckily, we happen to be one of the most funded and, institutionally, we are a younger organisation than most.

## Vision
The Indian FOSS & Digital Commons communities shape international discourse around FOSS & the Digital Commons.

That's a grand vision, but how do we realise it? Not-for-profit organisations are mission-driven, and our mission is to support and promote the Indian FOSS & Digital Commons ecosystems. But that's a broad mission statement. Over the past year, we have been able to refine it as follows -

## Mission
FOSS United Foundation supports and promotes the Indian FOSS ecosystem. We bring new people into existing communities, and we support the people already in the communities to sustain themselves. We incubate new communities when and where they are needed, and enable existing communities to thrive. We work with the industry to publicly acknowledge their usage of FOSS and help them support the Indian FOSS ecosystem. We do this by organising events, providing grants, and campaigning for public policy changes.

## Programs at FOSS United
Three distinct principal components emerge when you look at the mission statement - individuals, communities, and organisations - and three kinds of mini missions can be observed: bring new people into the ecosystem, help those already in the ecosystem to sustain themselves, and campaign for institutional support of the ecosystem. These three mini missions form the core of our Programs at FOSS United. A Programme or Program is a structured, funded initiative designed to solve a specific issue, and the "impact" of the Program can be measured in various ways. Therefore, we end up with three Programs at FOSS United - the Learners Program, the Maintainers Program, and the Campaigns Program. And it's incredible that all of our work already fits neatly into an overlap of these Programs.

If you are not aware, the FOSS United Foundation has carried out the following activities over our first five years

- IndiaFOSS annual festival, held in Bengaluru. The conference brings together technologists, designers, policy professionals, and more. It is a welcoming space both for young and inexperienced students and for old and experienced maintainers and creators.
- FOSS Hack annual hackathon, organized in a hybrid format. The hackathon brings together students and professionals from across the country to create new FOSS projects and to contribute to existing FOSS projects. In the most recent edition of the hackathon, the scope of the hackathon expanded from FOSS projects to Digital Commons projects, e.g., mapping, documentation, design, and other forms of contributions.
- Grants to financially support the creation of new FOSS. Broadly speaking, only a privileged few create and maintain FOSS, which unfortunately reduces the breadth of problems solved by the FOSS. By financially supporting people to create and maintain FOSS, we are enabling a wider variety of problems to be solved.
- City communities, incubated across the country. Usually formed with some form of support from the Foundation staff, the communities are usually made up of volunteers and organise events in the city to evangelise FOSS.
- Student clubs, organised across the country. With active support from the Foundation, student clubs evangelise FOSS within their college, and attempt to create new or contribute to existing FOSS projects. The clubs are degree- and department-agnostic to enable multi- and transdisciplinary work from the clubs.
- Public (Tech) policy awareness, primarily among creators and maintainers of FOSS. The Indian FOSS ecosystem is dictated by incentive structures that are unfortunately not visible to most of the people. Through our public (tech) policy work, we help the ecosystem develop a basic understanding of public (tech) policy, so that the ecosystem can advocate for policies that enable the sustenance of our ecosystem.
- Campaigns, aimed primarily towards the broader Software and Tech ecosystems. Practically every Indian software developer uses FOSS, but, unfortunately, most of them are unable to name an Indian FOSS creator or maintainer, and are unable to name prominent FOSS projects that are created and maintained from India. Our campaigns aim to change the cultural perception among young and inexperienced students and software developers, so that they recognise how India runs on FOSS, and who creates and maintains world-class FOSS from India.

Now, let's see how this work fits into the Programs I listed above - Learners Program, Maintainers Program, Campaigns Program.

- The Learners Program aims to build autonomy and agency within individuals, and the program helps the ecosystem develop a common, shared, foundational understanding of FOSS. To achieve this, the Learner Program works with the "uninitiated", i.e., college students, via Student Clubs, and professionals via city communities. The Program focuses on evangelising FOSS to help people understand the value of FOSS. The Program works with individuals, communities, and organisations.
- The Maintainers Program works to create a sustainable FOSS ecosystem, and the program helps the ecosystem fund, nurture, and support individuals who can provide the necessary care to the FOSS projects. To achieve this, the Maintainers Program works with the "initiated", i.e., contributors, maintainers, and creators of FOSS, irrespective of their age, experience, educational or professional background. The Program focuses on building capacity and enables knowledge sharing, to help creators and maintainers sustain FOSS. The Program works with individuals, communities, and organizations.
- The Campaigns Program attempts to shift the cultural perception of FOSS, and the program makes FOSS & the Digital Commons accessible.

### Improving an understanding of Tech Policy for FOSS developers

The Foundation continues to offer scholarships for FOSS developers to pursue the Graduate
Certificate in Public Policy (Tech Policy) course by the Takshashila Institution. At IndiaFOSS 2025,
more than a handful of the scholarship recipients came together during a Birds of a Feather session to discuss their experience with the 
program, and uninamously noted that FOSS United should continue providing the scholarship to
worthy applicants.

But we have also consistently observed that at least a few scholarship recipents are unable to
complete the course, for instance, they aren't able to submit the final assignment submission on
time. When we dug further, we realized that some of the recipients underestimated the time and
energy necessary to pursue the course, especially alongside a demanding job. We also realized
that the course wasn't well suited for early-career professionals, e.g., people who are one or
two years into their job. This is because their world-view lacks nuance, which unfortunately
only comes with time and experience.

When looking for ways to support early-career FOSS developers, we came across the Policy in Action Program (PAP) by
Young Leaders for Active Citizenship (YLAC). Ansh Arora from the team pursued the course, and noted that
the course was well suited for early career professionals, and that the shorter duration also made
it easier for someone to commit to the course. We have now started offering scholarships for FOSS
developers to pursue the PAP program as well, and we are actively trying to steer FOSS developers to
either of the courses, depending on their experience.

### FOSS Hack 2026

The judges were unequivocal after FOSS Hack 2025 - that FOSS Hack needed to change fundamentally.
Participants had easy access to code-generating AI models, and the judges had the difficult task
of sifting through (what we now call) AI slop. And change we did. In 2026, FOSS Hack went from
being a weekend-long hackathon to being a month-long hackathon, and it moved from focusing
exclusively on the code to supporting work across the Digital Commons, i.e., documentation,
testing, security, design, open mapping, and more. Looking at just the numbers, 5000+ participants
registered as 2000+ teams to participate in the event, working across 800 projects in the end,
an improvement from FOSS Hack 2025.

## Maintainers Program

FOSS United Foundation has been disbursing grants to deserving individuals, communities, and organisations since our inception in 2020. But the grant-giving was ad-hoc, and we started overhauling it in 2025. We started by dramatically expanding the budget for Grants - 25 lakh INR - and by speaking about our grants publicly. We also acknowledged that the grant-giving process was not binary - that it was not a simple yes or no. Most conversations with potential grantees, in fact, started with conversations about different levels of support that they could seek - starting with small infrastructure grants to cover their server costs, to small internship grants to cover summer student contributors, to fellowships that enable people to work on a project part-time, to large grants that support creators to work on a project full-time. Unfortunately, educated conversations about sustainability in the FOSS space are few and far between, and there is little to no information publicly available about different levels of financial support that our community members need.

Below, you will see projects, events, and fellowships that we financially supported in 2025, but we realised during the year that grants were not a program in themselves, but that grants were part of a larger program. As we talked to potential and existing grantees, we realised that the financial support only solved one of the problems - and that they still needed support to manage their project, gain and retain contributors, discover new users, UI/UX feedback, and more! And this is how the Maintainers Program came about - to fund, nurture, and support the individuals who provide care to the FOSS & Digital Commons ecosystems.

In 2025, we also became ecosystem partners for the [FLOSS/Fund](https://floss.fund/). As ecosystem partners, we were helping spread the word about FLOSS/Fund, and also with evaluating grant applications for the FLOSS/Fund.

For the full list of project grants by FOSS United in 2025, please see Appendix-C.

### CircuitVerse
What started as a message on Telegram turned into an infrastructure grant for the CircuitVerse project. Born out of IIIT B, CircuitVerse is a teaching tool designed for use in the higher education sector. The maintainers held stable jobs, and weren't interested in financial support to work on the project, but their server bills had started piling up, and were bring covered by individuals on the project. We offered them an infrastructure grant, which fit their needs, and helped us expand the imagination of funding in the FOSS ecosystem.

### Supporting the Nagarathna Memorial Grant by Thejesh G N
We quickly realised a problem with the Maintainers Program - the people who reached out to us already knew about us - and we weren't doing anything to ensure that we're supporting people who don't know us, and will likely never reach out to us directly. This could be because we aren't a known entity in their part of the Digital Commons, or that we aren't an "authority figure" in their part of the Digital Commons. The solution to this problem isn't to expand to every part of the Digital Commons, but rather to support the people who are already actively supporting people in these niches. And Thejesh is one such person.

If you don't know, Thejesh is one of the founders of the DataMeet community, an OG Data community and conference that happened in Bengaluru pre-pandemic. The community is still active, but it is primarily virtual. And we got to know that Thejesh has been running a micro-grants program, the Nagarathna Memorial Grant, to support projects in the Open Data and FOSS ecosystems. We dug a little further and came across Friends of the NMG Grant, people who put money into the grant pool. It was a no-brainer for us to become a Friend of the NMG Grant.

We are calling this the Lighthouse model - people like Thejesh are lighthouses in their respective niches of the Digital Commons. They have a natural gravity, and they have built up trust and reputation in their ecosystem thanks to their long-standing work. We are now trying to discover more Lighthouses and work with them to support their niches.

### Indian FOSS projects at FOSDEM
In case you didn't know, FOSDEM is the largest FOSS gathering in the world, held annually in Belgium. One of the FOSDEM volunteers was invited to speak at IndiaFOSS, and our booth applications at FOSDEM were accepted - one for the FOSS United Foundation and another for the FLOSS/Fund. International travel is a big deal for people But it is also a significant financial burden on an organisation, especially on a not-for-profit foundation. So we decided to experiment with something different at our first presence at FOSDEM - we decided to have community members represent us instead of being present at the booth ourselves.

A few things worked in our favour in this regard - Murtaza and Hussain, creators of Rethink DNS, were speaking at one of the FOSDEM devrooms, and Prasun, creator of Zasper, was speaking at another FOSDEM devroom. In addition, a few members of the Mecha team were also planning on participating in Mecha and were organising a parallel event during FOSDEM. Finally, one of our Governing Board members, Nemo, was coincidentally in Germany during the event, and intended to participate in the event. So, we came up with a plan - we would support members of the FOSS United Community to represent us at FOSDEM, and we would partially support their travel and stay. And Nemo, as a representative of the community, would coordinate.

In the end, this turned out well - FOSS United, community members, and FOSDEM happy with the experiment. FOSS United aims to promote and support the FOSS ecosystem in India - there wasn't a clear reason for us to be at FOSDEM, so we were incredibly happy to support our ecosystem to showcase their work on an international stage.

	- Most of the Indian FOSS ecosystem has an academic background in Computer Science. So, when we asked people what they thought colleges spent on software every year, and what was the costliest software they paid for, we usually heard responses in the 10 Lakhs INR order of magnitude and Google Workshop/Microsoft Windows being the costliest software. This is unfortunately an outcome of the narrow focus that most of the Indian education sector had on Computer Science, and the lack of awareness about non-computer science disciplines. We knew that engineering and simulation software such as Matlab, Ansys, and Comsol, were the primary expense items for Engineering colleges, and our realisation that much of the Indian software ecosystem didn't know this pushed us to start this campaign.
	- The first round of RTIs with NITs yielded mixed results - 9 of the NITs responded, with the other 12 rejecting our RTI application or responding with little to no information. But the 9 responses validated our understanding - multiple crores of Rupees were being spent every year on software subscriptions, with close to 70% of the expenditure going towards engineering and simulation software, e.g., Matlab, Comsol, Ansys, AutoCAD, and only 30% spent on administrative software, e.g., Tally, Microsoft Office suite, Google Workspace, Zoom.
	- In late 2025 and early 2026, we filed additional RTIs with IIITs, AIIMS, and IIMs. Please keep an eye on licencetolearn.in for updates.
- Maintainer May - 31 Maintainers in 31 Days https://forum.fossunited.org/t/meet-the-maintainers-31-days-31-foss-maintainers-from-india/5651
	- In late 2024, I started putting together a list of FOSS projects that were created in or maintained from India, and without using a search engine, I was able to put together a 100+
	- In 2025, I started asking software developers if they could name FOSS projects that were created or maintained in India, or if they could name Indians who were maintaining or creating FOSS projects from India. Unfortunately, most of the people I asked could only name one or two projects or individuals. Even in the best case scenario, people named a handful (5-10) projects or people, but not more.
	- Why is this a problem? Because this is unfortunately indicative of the cultural perception of FOSS in India. Most Indian software developers are indifferent to the FOSS that our work is built on top of. If you can't name FOSS projects that you use, or if you can't name who maintains or who created those projects, you mostly likely don't care about the sustainability of the projects either.
	- Worse, this limits the imagination of the Indian software development ecosystem. Every year, we run into thousands of students and early career professionals who express interest in contributing to FOSS, but they can't name more than one or two FOSS projects that they rely upon, directly or indirectly. And the few people that know a thing or two about FOSS projects and maintainers/creators, their role models are almost exclusively westerners.
	- With #MaintainerMay and Forklore, we wanted to change these perceptions. So we deliberately made a few decisions
		- the maintainers/creators come first, not the projects. This was because other listicles have been created in the past, for instance by GitHub, but the focus is usually on the projects from India. And it's not immediately obvious from a GitHub project who created it or who maintains it
		- limit the listicle to only include maintainers and creators who were in India, not Indians who are involved in FOSS work outside of India. This is because we wanted to demonstrate that world-class FOSS work was bring produced from India
		- finally, we added a few questions to make the people relatable, to hopefully convey the fact that there isn't anything mythical about FOSS creators and maintainers

### Diversity, Equity, and Inclusion

It's no surprise that there is little to no gender diversity in the software ecosystem,
let alone the FOSS ecosystem. Apart from gender, diversity in academic background, in
geographic location, and so on, are also a systemic issue in the tech sector. Last year,
we chose to expand the scope of our diversity efforts to go beyond only gender diversity.
This was not because the gender diversity problem was "solved", it was because we needed
more breathing room to see results, in the short-term and in the long-term.

Apart from Diversity Scholarships at IndiaFOSS 2025, our effort to improve vernacular
FOSS advocacy are worth calling out here. After ChennaiFOSS 2024, we slowly observed
an ever increasing discourse about FOSS in the Tamil language by the Chennai tech
ecosystem. The discourse didn't just stop with YouTubers - the Chennai tech ecosystem,
including the FOSS United Chennai community, organized the first ever
Tamil Open Source Software Conference, i.e., TOSSConf, in Chennai last year! It's the
first tech conference in our awareness where the primary language for communication
during the event wasn't English. Along the same lines, it's worth calling out Advays' and Aaravs'
efforts to advocate for Blender in Kannada, supported by a FOSS United grant.

It took a few years, but it has become abundantly clear to us that DEI work is,
and needs to be treated as, an enabler across the organization. But we are yet to meaningfully
incorporate DEI work across the breadth of work at FOSS United, let alone articulate
and achieve the goals we had for the Diversity Scholarships program at IndiaFOSS in the
first place. Our efforts to find long-term partners to work on improving diversity
have also been in vain, since most diversity scholarship recipients don't continue
engaging with the FOSS ecosystem after the event, as far as we are aware.

Over the coming year, we will layout the goals of our diversity work, evaluation rubrics
for the success or failure of our diversity work across the breadth of FOSS United initiatives,
and ensure that diversity is treated as an org-wide effort.

### Tech

Apart from regular upkeep and maintenance of the code and infrastructure behind
fossunited.org, the tech team mostly focused design-related changes to various
FOSS United pages, for instance, FOSS Hack 2026. The team also continues to maintain
the tech infrastructure that the FOSS United community has come to rely on, for instance,
Jitsi Meet for internal and open community calls, listmonk for newsletters, and so on.
For a complete understanding of the FOSS that we rely upon, both for fossunited.org
and other websites, and for internal team infrastructure, please see the
[FOSS United stack page](https://fossunited.org/stack).

While the software powering fossunited.org is FOSS, we only get a handful of contributions,
and almost all of the contributions are fly-by in nature. The advent of code-generating
AI has reduced the barrier for contribution, but it appears we have a long way
to go before we become a FOSS project that is not only open-source but is also
built and maintained by the FOSS United Community, and not just by the team.
Volunteers use the platform a few times a month, so volunteers are best suited
to comment on what is needed and what isn't. Over the coming year, we will
attempt to better understand what we can do to improve contributions to the
codebase..

Additionally, while the software is FOSS, there are no other users of the software,
as far as we are aware. And this isn't because there isn't a need for an event management
software - at least a few times an year, we come across people who built an event management
software from scratch, or people who are looking for a FOSS alternative only to end up
eventually on Luma. The FOSS United codebase has a long way to go before it's truly a
general purpose event management software, at least as far as the needs of a FOSS community
are. We intend to address this over the coming year - not because we want to become one more
general purpose event management software, but because it's almost good enough to solve the
needs of the people that reach out to us asking for solutions.
